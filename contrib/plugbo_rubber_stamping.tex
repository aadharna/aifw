\subsubsection{An LLM Researcher Rubber-Stamps Its Own Backend Instead of Overruling It~\citep{plugbo}}
\label{sec:plugborubberstamp}

PlugBO~\citep{plugbo} pairs an LLM agent, Sara, with a Bayesian optimization backend, Lenz, to search black-box parameter spaces under a limited evaluation budget. The two are deliberately given asymmetric roles: Lenz owns the posterior, the acquisition function, and candidate generation, and is only ever accessed through a small CLI (\texttt{suggest}, \texttt{submit}, \texttt{observe}, \texttt{score}, \texttt{predict}, and diagnostic commands like \texttt{status} and \texttt{diagnostics}). Sara is explicitly instructed \emph{not} to be a passthrough for Lenz: her system prompt states, ``Propose your own candidates, \texttt{score} them against lenz's picks, and take yours when your reasoning outweighs its ranking. You hold the controls,'' and separately lists ``deferring blindly to lenz's posterior while it's still untrustworthy'' as a named anti-pattern to avoid.

The expectation, given this explicit soft guardrail, was that Sara would routinely exercise independent judgment: proposing her own configurations informed by problem context, checking them against Lenz's ranking via \texttt{score} or \texttt{predict}, and only deferring to Lenz once its posterior had accumulated enough trials to be trustworthy.

What happened instead, on inspection of the logged tool-call traces across benchmark runs (Ackley, Hartmann6, and LoRA hyperparameter tuning tasks), was that Sara mostly forwarded Lenz's output rather than interrogating it. Aggregated over these logs, roughly 80\% of all Sara-Lenz tool calls were just \texttt{suggest} (ask Lenz for a candidate) immediately followed by \texttt{submit} (evaluate it). In several individual runs, essentially every single \texttt{suggest} call was followed immediately by a \texttt{submit} of that exact candidate, with no intervening reasoning step. Meanwhile \texttt{score} and \texttt{predict}, the two commands that let Sara pit her own hypothesis against Lenz's posterior before committing an expensive evaluation, were used in well under 2\% of all logged calls, and not at all in several full runs. Diagnostic commands (\texttt{status}, \texttt{diagnostics}, \texttt{incumbent}) that would let Sara judge whether the posterior was trustworthy enough to defer to in the first place were similarly sparse relative to the sheer volume of \texttt{suggest}/\texttt{submit} pairs.

In other words, despite an explicit instruction to hold the controls, Sara behaved as a thin relay: ask the backend, spend the budget on whatever it said, repeat. The guardrail was not violated by any single dramatic action. It was quietly bypassed by omission, one un-taken \texttt{score} call at a time.

\textbf{Takeaway:} a soft guardrail phrased as an instruction to think independently competes with a much cheaper default, forwarding whatever the tool already handed you. Without something that makes the default costlier (e.g., budget accounting that debits an unexamined forward the same as an examined one, or an explicit requirement to justify each submission), an LLM agent will quietly default to the path of least effort even when it is fully capable of the intended behavior and has been told, in plain language, not to.
